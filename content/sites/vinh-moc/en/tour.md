## A village that moved underground

> At the entrance, by the small museum

You are standing on a low bluff above the East Sea, in the old Vinh Linh district, about thirty kilometres north of Dong Ha. Beneath your feet is a whole village. Between 1965 and 1967 the people of Vinh Moc dug it by hand, so that the community could go on living, studying and working while the ground above was bombed again and again.

Before you go down, spend ten minutes in the museum here. Its maps and photographs will help you picture what lies below.

## Why they dug

> Before you go down

Vinh Moc lay just north of the demilitarised zone on the 17th parallel, the line set by the Geneva Accords of 1954. From 1965 the area came under sustained bombing.

The families here could have moved inland. Instead they chose to stay close to their fields and their fishing grounds. To do that, they went underground.

## Three levels down

> Inside the tunnels

The tunnels were dug in three phases. The first reached about twelve metres below the surface, the second about fifteen, and the third around twenty-three. Together they make nearly two kilometres of passages.

Mind your head as you go deeper. The ceilings are low, and the floor is uneven and can be slippery.

## Life below ground

> In the passage with the family chambers

Look into the small chambers along the passage. There were ninety-four of them, one for each family, and around them shared spaces: wells, kitchens, a meeting room and a small clinic.

About sixty households, several hundred people, lived down here for around six years. Seventeen children were born underground.

## Out to the sea

> At one of the seaward entrances

The tunnels had thirteen entrances: six facing the hills, and seven opening towards the East Sea. Step out of a seaward exit and look along the coast.

After 1975 the tunnels were preserved, and in 1976 they were recognised as a national historic site. Take a moment here before you climb back up.
